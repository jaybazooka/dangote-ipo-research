00

Executive Summary

Dangote Petroleum Refinery & Petrochemicals FZE is selling 4.1 billion new shares at ₦525 apiece, aiming to raise ₦2.15 trillion in what's being called Africa's largest-ever IPO. This brief comes from a direct read of the company's own prospectus, not the press coverage around it, and it tells a more complicated story than the headlines do.

The short version: two straight years of losses were followed by one genuinely strong half-year, funded almost entirely by continued expansion rather than debt relief, sold by a founder who will still control the board no matter how the retail offer goes, with dividends left entirely to that board's discretion. None of that makes it a bad investment, but it does make it a different one than "buy Nigeria's biggest, safest new blue-chip," which is roughly how it's being marketed.

The financial track record is one good half, not a trend. DPRP lost ₦2.23 trillion in 2024 and ₦723 billion in 2025, then swung to a ₦2.50 trillion profit in H1 2026 alone, right after the refinery finally reached stable full-capacity output. A large part of the debt behind that story was owed to the founder himself: of the $6.24 billion outstanding at the end of 2025, $3.99 billion was an unsecured loan from Dangote Industries Limited, the controlling shareholder, not a bank. By mid-2026 total debt stood at $5.67 billion, now fully secured.

Every naira raised in this offer goes toward growth rather than safety. All of it funds a five-year, roughly $14.3 billion expansion to 1.4 million barrels a day, and none of it retires debt. Aliko Dangote keeps the wheel regardless, holding 87.27% of the company beneficially before the offer even opens. Dividends remain a maybe on paper: the board decides if, when, and how much, and loan covenants can override that decision entirely. And a tax exemption on domestic sales narrows from 1 January 2028, squarely inside the window this expansion is funding.

01

The Offer, Plainly

The mechanics are straightforward. 4.1 billion ordinary shares go on sale at a fixed ₦525, with a minimum ticket of 10 shares (₦5,250), deliberately low to invite mass retail participation. Applications open 14 September and close 13 October 2026, with listing on the Nigerian Exchange expected in November. Twenty-four issuing houses, led by Vetiva Advisory Services, are distributing the deal, which tells you how central this listing is to Nigeria's capital markets right now.

Read plainly: the company lost ₦2.23 trillion in its first full revenue year, then another ₦723 billion in 2025, despite ₦18.7 trillion of sales that year. Then, in the six months to June 2026, it made ₦2.5 trillion, more than the two prior years lost, combined. That turnaround is real. It followed the refinery finally hitting stable full-capacity operation, but it's one clean profitable stretch, not an established pattern investors can extrapolate confidently from.

Petrol now drives the business

Revenue mix has shifted hard toward PMS, or petrol, which has gone from a minor product line to the largest single revenue source in under two years, and along the way DPRP has become the dominant domestic supplier.

As of May 2026, the prospectus says DPRP supplied 100% of domestically-produced PMS and roughly 87.6% of Nigeria's total PMS supply once imports are counted in. That level of market share matters beyond the headline number: it's the kind of dominance in the country's most politically sensitive fuel that tends to draw regulatory attention, worth keeping in mind alongside the numbers.

How the profit swing happened

Cost of sales rose from ₦8.41tn to ₦15.70tn between H1 2025 and H1 2026, up 86.6%, but revenue grew faster, which is the entire story of the gross profit swing from ₦225bn to ₦3.43tn. Fixed and semi-fixed costs that used to swamp a smaller production base are now being spread across a much larger one. It's a genuine operating-leverage story, and it's also exactly why the case depends on utilisation and margins holding up.

03

The Debt Question

Press coverage of this IPO has cited debt figures anywhere from $3.65bn to $9bn, which is confusing enough to be worth resolving directly against the prospectus itself.

Net debt / EBITDA—0.27x

The detail that tends to get lost: at the end of 2025, nearly two-thirds of total debt ($3.99bn of $6.24bn) was an unsecured loan from Dangote Industries Limited, the controlling shareholder, not third-party bank debt. By mid-2026 that balance had rolled into fully secured facilities, most likely as part of the $4bn syndicated refinancing arranged with Afreximbank and Access Bank earlier in the year. So the debt reduction you'll see quoted in headlines is real, but it's better understood as a restructuring of who the company owes, not simply "debt getting paid off." The $3.65bn figure circulating in some outlets doesn't appear anywhere in the prospectus, so treat the $5.67bn audited figure as the reliable one.

Borrowing costs are disclosed precisely: bank and working-capital loans price at SOFR + 6 to 7%, term loans at SOFR + 5%, for an all-in cost somewhere around 9 to 12% at current rates. A 200-basis-point move in rates would swing H1 2026 profit by roughly ±$113 million on its own. DPRP also doesn't manage its own commodity price risk. That's handled entirely by parent company Dangote Industries Limited through a back-to-back derivative arrangement, one more thread of financial dependence on the founder's holding company beyond debt and ownership.

However well the IPO goes, control isn't up for negotiation. Aliko Dangote's combined beneficial stake sits at 87.27% before a single new share is sold.

Worth noting: NNPC's stake landed well below the 20% originally floated when the refinery was first being financed. After the new shares dilute the register, Dangote's control settles closer to the ~84% figure that's circulated in the press, still a level that leaves minority shareholders with essentially no influence over strategy, related-party dealing, or whether a dividend ever gets declared.

05

How ₦525 Was Arrived At

Two valuation approaches sit behind the offer price. Chapel Hill Denham blended a discounted cash flow model (50%) with EV/EBITDA (25%) and P/E multiples (25%) to reach roughly $62.53bn. Renaissance Capital ran a seven-year DCF at a 13.3% WACC and 2.6% terminal growth rate, explicitly building in upside from the Phase 2 expansion. Some sell-side estimates go further still, putting fair value as high as ₦77.7–82.6 trillion, notably above the ₦65.22tn implied by the listing price.

Worth sitting with: the offer price was set by the Issuer in consultation with the same Issuing Houses distributing the deal. It is not an independent third-party valuation. Analysts producing bullish numbers ahead of a listing they're helping sell have an obvious incentive problem, and their estimates should be read with that in mind, not taken at face value.

Where the Money Actually Goes

None of it pays down debt. Every naira of net proceeds is earmarked for Phase 2: a five-year, roughly $14.3 billion programme targeted for completion in 2029 that aims to roughly double refining capacity, from 700,000 to 1.4 million barrels a day.

In other words: this isn't capital going into a mature, de-risked cash generator. It's capital going into a second construction cycle on top of a business that only recently proved it could run its first phase profitably.

07

What the Company Itself Warns About

The prospectus runs to roughly twenty pages of risk factors. Most of it is standard boilerplate, but a meaningful chunk is specific, current, and worth knowing before you commit money.

The company itself

The disclosures single out how young this operation still is at scale. The rerated 700,000 bpd capacity was only proven in June 2026, so the entire investment case currently rests on two or three months of operating history at the level that matters. All of that capacity sits on one site, a single 2,635-hectare coastal plot holding every refining, power, and storage function with no backup location, and the company names flooding, fire, and sabotage directly as risks tied to that concentration. The refinery also runs its own captive power plant with no grid fallback, so any fault there halts production directly. Phase 2 carries real execution risk too: the original build took about seven years against a shorter initial plan, and the prospectus states plainly that the expansion "may not be completed on time, within budget, or at all." Fourteen legal cases are pending, nine of them material, totalling roughly ₦4.08bn plus $216.1m in claims. The company's solicitors consider an adverse outcome unlikely to be material, a normal disclosure at this scale and nothing that stands out as alarming.

The industry

Two industry-wide risks are worth flagging too. Global refining overcapacity in the Middle East and Asia could compress the margins the entire recent profit story depends on, and the long-run energy transition, meaning EVs, biofuels, and tightening plastics regulation, is named directly as a threat to both fuel and petrochemical demand.

Nigeria, and everything outside the fence line

Nigeria supplies most of the real risk here. NUPRC reported 485 pipeline sabotage incidents in 2024 alone, and Nigeria lost an estimated 13.5 million barrels, worth about $3.3bn, to theft and sabotage across 2023 and 2024. Sector labour action has already shown it can move the needle: a September 2025 strike cut national crude output by roughly 16%, or 283,000 barrels a day. The naira has been genuinely volatile too, falling from ₦666.67/$ in 2023 to a low near ₦1,717.50/$ in November 2024 before settling into a ₦1,340 to ₦1,445 band through H1 2026. Inflation isn't solved either: it eased to 15.06% in February 2026, then ticked back up to 15.69% in April, which the prospectus itself partly blames on a regional energy-price shock and shipping disruption through the Strait of Hormuz.

The regulatory ground is also shifting under the company's feet. The Petroleum Industry Act is actively being rewritten, disclosed in February 2026 with the scope of the amendments not yet public, and fuel subsidy policy has reversed three times since May 2023: removed, partially reinstated, then removed again, which the company itself names as a direct source of pricing unpredictability. Trade access to the US is unsettled, since AGOA lapsed on 30 September 2025 and was reauthorised only to 31 December 2026, against a backdrop of shifting US tariffs and surcharges on Nigerian exports. Nigeria ranked 142nd of 182 countries on Transparency International's 2025 corruption index, disclosed candidly in the prospectus. And a tax cliff looms: the free-zone exemption on domestic sales narrows from 1 January 2028, landing squarely inside the Phase 2 spending window and hitting a revenue line, PMS, that's now 42% of the business.

The offer itself

A few risks are specific to the shares rather than the business. They're US-dollar denominated in the company's own books but will trade in naira on the NGX, an extra currency layer to track. There's no independent guarantee the offer price holds once trading starts, and with a thin free float, liquidity for anyone wanting to exit is a genuinely open question. Future share issuance also remains possible and could dilute today's buyers.

08

Dividends and the Real Long-Term Case

Here's the sentence that should be read twice before anyone buys this expecting income: "the declaration, payment and amount of any future dividends will be at the discretion of the Issuer's Board of Directors," and dividends "may be restricted by the terms of its financing arrangements, including financial covenants." No payout ratio. No start date. Nothing binding.

That reframes what a long-term holder is actually betting on. Without reliable income, the return case rests almost entirely on the share price going up, which depends on the following holding true.

  • Phase 2 landing close to its 2029 target, on budget, at 1.4 million bpd.
  • Refining margins holding near, or improving on, the current $24.50 per barrel level.
  • Institutional and index-tracking demand once DPRP becomes the NGX's single largest listed company.
  • The one-off bonus share for twelve-month holders, which is real but a modest sweetener rather than a compounding return.
Working for the case
  • There's a genuine operating-leverage story here once full capacity is reached and sustained.
  • The USD-functional earnings base offers some insulation from naira depreciation over the long run.
  • A dominant, near-monopoly domestic PMS position gives real pricing power in Nigeria.
  • Dangote Cement, a related but separate entity, has a track record of paying consistent dividends, which at least shows precedent within the wider group.
Working against it
  • Insider ownership above 87% means minority holders can't push for a dividend even if they want one.
  • Loan covenants can legally block payouts regardless of what the board might prefer.
  • The 2028 tax cliff lands on PMS revenue just as expansion capex peaks.
  • There's zero trading history for this specific stock on a thin free float, so the liquidity needed to actually realise a capital gain is untested.
09

Bottom Line

Without a reliable dividend, this is functionally a growth-and-expansion equity bet on Aliko Dangote successfully executing a second multi-billion-dollar buildout, wrapped in Nigeria country risk and a global refining cycle he doesn't control. The upside is real if Phase 2 lands close to plan and margins hold. But it's a materially different, higher-risk proposition than "buying a stable Nigerian dividend blue-chip," which is roughly how some of the coverage around this listing has framed it.
